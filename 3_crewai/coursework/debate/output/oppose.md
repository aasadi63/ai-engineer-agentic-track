I oppose the motion.

**LLMs should not be subject to restrictive laws because overregulation would do more harm than good.** These tools are not just products; they are general-purpose technologies that can be used for education, productivity, healthcare support, creativity, and economic growth. If we impose heavy-handed restrictions now, we risk freezing innovation, favoring large corporations that can afford compliance, and denying smaller developers, researchers, and the public access to the benefits of AI.

The key point is this: **the harms of LLMs are real, but restrictive laws are a blunt instrument.** Bad actors will still misuse AI whether laws exist or not. What restrictive regulation often does is burden legitimate users, slow safety improvements, and push development into less transparent, less regulated spaces. That makes the problem worse, not better.

We should also be careful not to treat LLMs like ordinary dangerous goods. Unlike medicine or cars, LLMs are flexible tools with countless lawful uses. A one-size-fits-all legal framework would likely be outdated almost immediately and unable to keep pace with the technology. **Responsible self-regulation, transparent industry standards, independent audits, and targeted enforcement against actual misuse are better solutions than broad restrictive laws.**

Finally, **innovation itself is a public good.** These systems can help students learn, assist doctors, support disabled users, and increase access to knowledge worldwide. If we overregulate too early, we lock in the power of a few incumbents and prevent society from realizing those benefits.

So the real choice is not “safety or chaos.” It is **smart, flexible oversight versus restrictive laws that stifle progress and concentrate power.** For that reason, the motion should be rejected.
